# The Legacy of Dr. John T. Hallfrisch

> *"If it can be put together, it can be taken apart."*

---

### To my children:

I want you to know that the life we have today is good because your grandfather worked incredibly hard for it yesterday. He didn't just "exist"; he built the foundation we stand on.

### The Philosophy of Time
Grandpa was an Emergency Room doctor, but his real full-time job was being a father. He treated time like a bank account—he was willing to "spend" all his energy to buy freedom for us. He would work 24-hour shifts, or bunch his shifts together and sleep at the hospital, just so he could save up weeks of vacation time.

He did this so we could take month-long trips in the motorhome. He traded his own sleep and comfort to give us the world. Even on those trips, he was on a mission—he’d drive 24 hours straight to Florida, nonstop, just to make sure we didn't waste a single day of vacation on the road.

### The Uniform ("Clothes Make the Man")
You might picture an ER doctor in scrubs, but that wasn't your grandfather. He refused to wear them. He believed in a suit and tie. He always cited studies showing that patients trusted a doctor in a tie more than one in pajamas.

But because he was always working, those nice clothes took a beating. It wasn't unusual to find him under the hood of a car or fixing a project while still wearing his dress slacks. He even had a specific pair of "project slacks"—dress pants that had been retired to the garage. On his rare days off, he’d switch to blue jeans and a button-up, often wearing his well-worn leather bomber jacket. He looked like a man ready for business, no matter what the business was.

### The Family Doctor & The Debater
We were spoiled having him as a father. I didn't know what a waiting room looked like until 2021. If we broke a bone, we went to Dad.

But it wasn't just us—he was the community's safety net. Long before he built the formal office in Marinette, his "clinic" was actually the third floor of our house. He had medical equipment set up right there in our home. When workers at the local factories got injured, they didn't always go to the ER—they came to our house. He would treat them upstairs, stitching cuts and checking injuries. Building the official office years later didn't change who he was; it just gave a formal address to the work he had been doing for our neighbors and local workers all along.

He was just as sharp politically. On those long commutes, the radio was always tuned to NPR. He knew the price of oil and the state of the world, and he loved to debate. He had a memory for facts and data that made him impossible to beat in an argument—he especially loved teasing Republicans with hard numbers.

He wasn't big on movies unless they were "Two Thumbs Up" award-winners with historical significance. We always joked about his high standards... although he did have a secret soft spot for the movie *Demolition Man*.

### The Teacher & The Builder
Grandpa didn't just tell us to work hard; he made us part of the work. Around the year 2000, he decided to open his own family practice in Marinette to make his work official. He didn't just hire a crew; he bought the building, designed the layout, and built it himself.

Once it was open, we weren't just kids; we were the staff. We cleaned, managed the office, and ran the phones. He created these projects to teach us that nothing is "above" you.

He passed down two main philosophies that ruled our house.

First, he believed that **knowledge is the only thing you truly own.** He would always say, *"Nobody can take your education from you or the things you learned."* He believed that material things could be lost, but your mind was your fortress.

Second, he taught us practical confidence. He used a saying from his own father: *"If it can be put together, it can be taken apart."*

That combination—valuing your education and not being afraid to get his hands dirty—is why I know how to fix my truck and why I can solve problems at my job today. He taught us that the world isn't a mystery; it’s just a puzzle waiting to be solved.

### Camp Life (Relaxation is Work)
When we went up to camp, we didn't go to sit around. For Grandpa, "relaxing" meant sitting on a tractor. We spent our time cutting shooting lanes into the swamp for deer season or building something.

Even on holidays like Labor Day, when we tried to plan a relaxing family picnic, he would find a project for us to do. Grandma, who raised six of us and ran the house while he was working those long shifts, would be right there making lunch to keep the work crew fed.

### The Heart & The Humor
For all that hard work, Grandpa knew how to enjoy the moment. He loved to socialize with a drink in hand and tell jokes. He had an incredible memory for them, especially the old "Toivo and Eino" stories. He loved to tease Grandma with jokes that were just a little bit "risky," just to get a rise out of her.

But my favorite image of him is with a book. He loved reading to us when we were kids, and he did the exact same thing for you, Drew. He treated his grandkids just like he treated us—with the same deep love, the same tractor rides, and the same expectation that you would grow up to be capable, strong people.

### The Quiet Acts of Love
As a kid, I used to get annoyed on our motorhome trips when Grandpa would come around in the middle of the night to straighten our blankets and tuck us in. I was getting older, I was usually too warm, and it frustrated me. 

But recently, as I was straightening the blanket over you as you slept to make sure you were comfortable, it hit me. I realized what Grandpa was actually doing all those years ago. He was checking on us. He was making sure we were safe, warm, and resting well. It was his quiet way of showing how deeply he cared about us. I never got to say thank you to him for those little things, but I want you to know that every time I tuck you in, I am carrying on his exact same love for you.

### The Unstoppable Engine
I want you to understand just how tough he was. Even at the end of his career, after leaving the ER in 2020, he refused to stop. He took a job at the VA in Iron Mountain, pulling 5 to 7 days in a row of 12-hour shifts. Since there was no place to sleep there, he created a network of "safe harbors"—sleeping at our camp in Stephenson or at Pinecrest in Powers—just to keep going.

In the months before his passing, he was pushing harder than ever. He was building the second pole barn at camp with your Uncle JJ. He would work a full shift at the VA, drive to camp, and immediately start framing trusses, sometimes on zero sleep.

He pushed himself so hard that his body eventually forced him to stop; his first stroke happened right there at camp, while he was working. He gave everything he had, quite literally, until he couldn't lift a hammer anymore.

### His Legacy
He worked this hard so you wouldn't have to worry about the basics. He would want you to be happy and free, but he would also want you to remember the value of grit. We miss him, but we are grateful, because we see his handiwork in everything we have.
