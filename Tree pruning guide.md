# Comprehensive Tree Pruning Guide

Pruning a tree is essentially creating a deliberate wound. The ultimate goal of proper pruning is to remove unwanted growth in a way that allows the tree to compartmentalize (seal) the wound quickly, preventing decay and disease. 

This guide serves as a refresher on pruning anatomy, proper cutting techniques, weather considerations, and rules for specific tree types.

---

## 1. Tree Anatomy: Where to Make the Cut

The most common and destructive mistake in pruning is the "flush cut" (cutting flat against the trunk). To avoid this, you must identify two crucial anatomical features before your saw ever touches the wood.

![[visiblecollar 1.gif]]

![[No-Visible-Collar(1) 1.jpg]]

* **The Branch Bark Ridge (The Top):** Look in the "V" (crotch) where the branch meets the trunk. You will see a raised, often darker, pushed-up line of bark. 
* **The Branch Collar (The Bottom/Sides):** Look at the base of the branch. It will have a swollen, flared "shoulder" where it attaches to the trunk. You may also see wrinkled or folded bark here.

**The Golden Rule:** *Never cut into the ridge or the collar.* These areas contain the specialized cells the tree needs to grow woundwood (a "scab") over the cut. 

**The Cut Line:** Draw an imaginary line starting just *outside* the Branch Bark Ridge, angling downward to exit just *outside* the swelling of the Branch Collar.

## 2. The 3-Cut Method (For Large Branches)

Never try to cut a heavy branch with a single cut. The weight will cause the branch to snap and peel a long strip of bark down the main trunk, causing severe damage. Always use the 3-Cut Method:

![[Proper-Tree-Pruning.jpg]]

1.  **The Undercut:** Go 6 to 12 inches away from the trunk. Cut exactly one-third of the way *up* from the bottom of the branch. (This stops the bark from peeling).
2.  **The Relief Cut:** Go another 1 to 2 inches further out from the trunk. Cut all the way through the branch from the top down. The branch will fall safely, breaking cleanly at your undercut.
3.  **The Final Cut:** Now that you only have a lightweight stub, locate your branch bark ridge and collar. Make a smooth, angled cut just outside these markers to remove the stub. 

**Special Note for Vertical/Co-dominant Stems:** If you are removing a vertical trunk (like the one that caused the apple tree rot), follow the same 3-cut method to remove the weight first. When making the final cut at the base, ensure the cut is **angled downward** (like a pitched roof) away from the remaining trunk so water sheds off immediately. Never leave a flat "tabletop" cut.

## 3. Weather and Timing: When to Prune

When you prune is just as important as how you prune. Fungal spores and bacteria rely on moisture to travel and infect fresh tree wounds. 

### The "Dry Spell" Rule
**It is generally considered very safe and highly recommended to prune during a dry spell.** If you check the forecast and see that it will be dry for the next week or two, this is an excellent window for tree work. 

* **Why?** Fresh cuts take time to dry out and begin sealing. If you prune right before a multi-day rainstorm, water will wash fungal spores directly into the fresh, open sapwood. Pruning during a dry stretch ensures the surface of the cut dries out quickly, creating a harsh environment for pathogens.

### Seasonal Pruning Rules
* **Late Winter (Dormant Season) - BEST:** The absolute best time to prune most trees. The tree is asleep, sap is not flowing, and bare branches make it easy to see the structure. When spring arrives, the tree throws all its energy into healing the cuts.
* **Summer - OKAY:** Good for minor shaping, thinning, or removing dead/broken branches. Since the tree is actively growing, it can seal wounds, but avoid heavy structural pruning as it removes food-producing leaves.
* **Fall - AVOID:** The worst time to prune. Decay fungi spread their spores profusely in the fall, and the tree is going to sleep, meaning it will not heal the wound until the following spring.
* **Spring Leaf-Out - AVOID:** The tree is expending massive amounts of energy pushing out new leaves. Pruning now stresses the tree and causes heavy sap bleeding.

## 4. Guidelines by Tree Type

Different trees have slightly different requirements and risks.

### Fruit Trees (Apples, Pears, Cherries, Peaches)
* **Goal:** Airflow and sunlight penetration. You want an open canopy so fruit can ripen and leaves can dry quickly (preventing mildew).
* **Timing:** Late winter/early spring before the buds swell.
* **Notes:** Fruit trees are highly susceptible to rot (as seen in the photo). Proper angled cuts outside the collar are non-negotiable. 

### Deciduous Shade Trees (Maples, Birches, etc.)
* **Goal:** Strong structure. Remove crossing branches, branches growing inward, or narrow V-shaped crotches.
* **Timing:** Late winter. 
* **Notes:** Maples and Birches will "bleed" heavy sap if pruned in late winter/early spring. This looks alarming but is generally harmless to the tree. 

### Evergreens and Conifers (Pines, Spruces, Firs)
* **Goal:** Minimal pruning. Only prune to remove dead, diseased, or hazardous branches. 
* **Timing:** Late winter (for Spruces/Firs). 
* **Notes:** Evergreens do not heal from old wood the way deciduous trees do. If you cut a branch back to the trunk, it will never grow back. 

### Special Warning: Oaks and Elms
* **The Rule:** NEVER PRUNE OAKS OR ELMS IN SPRING, SUMMER, OR EARLY FALL.
* **Why:** These trees are highly susceptible to fatal diseases (Oak Wilt and Dutch Elm Disease) spread by beetles that are attracted to the smell of fresh tree sap. 
* **Exception:** Only prune these trees in the dead of winter (December to February). If an emergency summer cut must be made (e.g., storm damage), you must immediately paint the wound with pruning sealer or latex paint to hide the scent from beetles (this is the *only* time pruning paint is recommended).

## 5. Tool Maintenance and Sanitation

* **Keep it Sharp:** A dull saw crushes and shreds bark rather than cutting it cleanly. Jagged, shredded cuts take much longer to heal.
* **Sanitize:** If you are pruning a tree that looks diseased, sanitize your saw or pruners before moving to the next tree. You can wipe the blades with 70 percent rubbing alcohol or a 10 percent bleach solution to avoid spreading pathogens.
